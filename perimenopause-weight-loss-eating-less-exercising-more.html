Perimenopause Weight Loss: Why Eating Less and Exercising More Can Stop Working

If weight loss feels different after 40, you are not necessarily doing something wrong. Sometimes the strategy needs to change—not your effort.

By Sushma · Eat Well Live Well

You may have noticed something has changed.

You are eating more or less the same way you always have. You may even be eating less. You are walking, exercising and being more careful about your portions. And yet the weight isn't coming off the way it used to.

Or you lose a few kilos and then gain them back. Your waist seems to be changing even though the weighing scale hasn't moved much.

And you start wondering, “What am I doing wrong?”

This is one of the most frustrating things I hear from women in their 40s.

Often, the answer isn't that you need to try harder. It may simply be that the strategy that worked in your 20s or 30s needs to change.

Why can weight loss feel different after 40?

There isn't one single reason.

As we move through our 40s, several things can change at the same time. Muscle mass can gradually decline if we aren't doing regular resistance training. Everyday movement may decrease without us noticing. Sleep can become more disrupted, stress can increase and appetite can feel different.

And during perimenopause and menopause, hormonal changes can also affect body composition and where fat is stored.

So when a woman says, “I haven't changed anything. Why is my body changing?”, I don't want to immediately tell her to eat less. I want to look at the whole picture.

The problem with eating less and less

This is where many women get stuck.

The scale goes up, so they cut something. Less rice. No sweets. Smaller portions. Skip breakfast. Stop eating after 7 pm. Then they add more exercise.

For a while, it may work. But eventually hunger catches up. You are tired, thinking about food all the time and by evening you are ravenous. Then comes the overeating, the guilt and another Monday of starting again.

Eat less. Exercise more. Start again.

This cycle is exhausting.

If your plan leaves you constantly hungry and unable to live your normal life, it isn't a very useful long-term strategy. Weight management is not simply about creating a bigger and bigger calorie deficit. It is also about building a way of eating and moving that you can actually sustain.

You don't have to remove the food you love

Let's take rice.

If you are an Indian woman who has eaten rice all your life, suddenly being told, “Stop eating rice if you want to lose weight,” isn't a very practical solution.

Rice isn't the problem. The bigger question is what the whole meal looks like.

Is there a meaningful source of protein? Are there vegetables and fibre? Are you eating slowly enough to notice when you're satisfied? Is the portion appropriate for you?

A meal of rice, vegetables, dal, curd and another protein source is very different from a large plate of rice with very little protein, followed by hunger again a couple of hours later.

You don't necessarily need to remove your familiar foods. You may need to build the meal differently.

Protein matters

One of the first things I look at with women over 40 is protein.

Not because protein is a magic weight-loss food. It isn't. But it helps support muscle and can make meals more satisfying.

Many women aren't eating no protein. They are simply eating most of it at one meal.

Breakfast might be tea and biscuits, two idlis or dosa. Lunch might be mostly rice and vegetables with a little dal. Dinner finally has paneer, eggs, chicken, fish or another substantial protein source.

Instead of asking only, “How can I eat less?”, start asking, “Where is my protein in this meal?”

Look at breakfast, lunch and dinner. You don't have to change everything overnight.

And then there is muscle

When women want to lose weight, they often focus almost entirely on burning calories. More walking. More cardio. Longer workouts.

Movement is absolutely valuable. But your body also needs a reason to maintain muscle. That's where strength training comes in.

You don't need to become a bodybuilder or lift extremely heavy weights. Regularly challenging your muscles can help support strength and muscle mass as you age.

Think beyond calories burned during a workout. Think about what you want your body to be able to do ten years from now: carry your groceries, climb stairs, get up from the floor, travel, lift your suitcase and stay independent.

Strength is not just about how many calories you burn. It is about the body you are building for the years ahead.

Exercise isn't only about your workout

Your formal workout might be one hour. But what happens during the other 23?

You sit at your desk, drive, sit through meetings and come home exhausted. Everyday movement can quietly drop without you realising it.

Maybe you used to walk around more, take the stairs or simply spend more time on your feet.

So don't think only about how many calories you burned during your workout. Think about how much you are moving throughout your day.

A short walk after a meal, getting up regularly during work, walking while talking on the phone or taking the stairs when practical can all help you become more active without adding another exhausting workout to your schedule.

Sleep and stress matter too

If you aren't sleeping well, everything can feel harder.

You wake up tired, have less energy to exercise and may feel hungrier during the day. When you're already juggling work, family and everything else life brings, making thoughtful choices becomes harder.

The same goes for stress.

Many women tell me, “I know what I should eat. But by evening, I just don't have the energy to care.”

I understand that.

If you've spent the entire day working, managing a home, looking after children or parents and solving everyone's problems, food can become the one thing you look forward to.

Instead of telling yourself you need more willpower, look at what happened earlier in the day. Did you eat enough at lunch? Did you have protein? Did you go too long without eating? Did you sleep badly?

Weight management doesn't happen separately from your life.

And yes, perimenopause matters

I don't want to dismiss the role of hormonal changes either.

Perimenopause is a real transition. Changes in hormones can affect body composition and fat distribution, and many women notice their bodies changing during this stage.

But I also don't want to tell you, “It's all your hormones. There's nothing you can do.”

That isn't helpful either.

Your hormones are part of the picture. So are your food choices, muscle, movement, sleep, stress, habits, medications and health conditions when relevant.

We need to look at the whole woman, not just one hormone.

So what should you focus on instead?

If you're trying to lose weight during perimenopause, I would start with the basics.

  • Put protein into your meals. Look at breakfast, lunch and dinner and ask where your protein is. Use foods you already eat—dal, beans, curd, paneer, tofu, eggs, fish, chicken or other suitable protein sources.
  • Keep your familiar foods. You don't have to stop eating rice, dosa or chapati. Look at how you can balance your plate instead.
  • Strength train. Give your muscles a reason to stay strong and gradually build from whatever level is appropriate for you.
  • Keep everyday movement high. Walk, take movement breaks, get outside and find ways to spend a little more time on your feet.
  • Pay attention to sleep. If your sleep is consistently poor, don't simply accept it as something you have to live with.
  • Don't ignore hunger. If you're constantly hungry, don't automatically assume you need more discipline. Look at whether your meals are satisfying, whether you're getting enough protein and fibre, whether you're going too long between meals and how you're sleeping.

Sometimes hunger is useful information.

What I would not do

I wouldn't start by cutting out rice completely, skipping meals to “save calories” or doing hours of cardio because you ate more yesterday.

I also wouldn't try to copy exactly what worked for you ten years ago.

Instead, step back and ask:

What does my body actually need more of?

More protein? More strength? More everyday movement? Better sleep? More structure around meals? Less restriction?

Sometimes the answer isn't “try harder.”

Sometimes it is “try differently.”

A simple place to start this week

Don't change everything tomorrow. For the next seven days, simply notice five things:

  • Where was my protein at breakfast?
  • Did I build a balanced lunch?
  • Did I move throughout the day?
  • Did I give my muscles some resistance?
  • What time did I start winding down for sleep?

You don't need to fix every gap immediately. Choose one thing you can improve.

Maybe it's adding curd or eggs to breakfast. Maybe it's taking a 10-minute walk after lunch. Maybe it's doing two short strength sessions this week. Maybe it's getting to bed 30 minutes earlier.

Small changes that you can actually repeat are much more useful than a perfect plan you abandon after five days.

One more important point

Not every change in weight or body composition during your 40s is something you should solve by changing your diet.

If you have unexpected or rapid weight changes, significant fatigue, persistent sleep problems or other new symptoms, speak with your doctor. If you have a medical condition or take medication that may affect your weight, appetite or metabolism, get individual medical advice rather than trying to solve everything through food and exercise.

How I approach weight management with women 40+

At Eat Well Live Well, I don't want women to spend the next decade moving from one restrictive diet to another.

I don't want you to be afraid of rice. I don't want you to feel guilty when you eat something you enjoy. And I don't want exercise to become punishment for what you ate.

I want you to understand your food, your hunger and how to build a satisfying Indian meal. I want you to understand the role of protein, strength, sleep and movement—and then build habits that fit your actual life.

Because your goal isn't simply to weigh less. It is to feel stronger, healthier and more confident in your body—and to build habits you can still live with years from now.
